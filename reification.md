### The outputs of a statistical model are statements about the model, not about the real world.

This principle refers to the fallacy of *statistical reification*, as defined by Sander Greenland (2017).  A more general form of this fallacy is discussed by Erica Thompson (2022); for a more succinct discussion, see Thompson & Smith (2019).

This is related to a legitimate debate within the philosophy of science, between *realists* and *instrumentalists*.  Both sides should be taken seriously:  I argue that the flowering of quantum information science in recent decades is a direct consequence of a small number of physicists who took research in the "foundations of quantum theory" seriously when it was a fringe subject, as opposed to the dominant "shut up and calculate" mentality that ruled for several generations (Mermin, 1989).   In physics and other sciences, the whole point of discovering "physical laws" is to extrapolate them -- to stress test how broadly their validity could be.

Statistical models, on the other hand, are usually purely empirical models of the data generating process: elaborate curve-fits, if you will.  March (1970) rightly observed that "as [Max] Planck fully realized, finding an empirical formula, one that fits the data but is not based on a physical picture of the process being described, can never be an end in itself in physics."  By contrast, statistical models are not meant for extrapolation because there is no empirical basis to extrapolate them.  Unlike the theories of other sciences, where a legitimate debate over their "reality" could be made, statistical models must *always* be entertained solely from an instrumentalist viewpoint.
